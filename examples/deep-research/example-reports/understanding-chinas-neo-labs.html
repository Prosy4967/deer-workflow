Executive brief

As of July 2026, "Neo Lab" is still not a category with stable boundaries in China, but rather an expanding investment and organizational label. In the narrow sense, it refers to researcher-led startup labs that have not yet achieved product-market fit but rely on long-term capital betting on breakthroughs in foundational technology; in the broad sense, it is also used to summarize companies such as Zhipu, DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, and MiniMax, in which research and product are tightly coupled. The existing evidence supports the claim that "China is forming research-driven AI organizations," but it is not sufficient to prove that China already possesses a mature, homogeneous, and replicable cohort of Neo Labs. The most reasonable attitude toward this trend is to affirm the potential of the research–engineering–product loop while refusing to let financing, valuation, and talent résumés substitute for verification of technical originality, user value, and capital efficiency.

6 evidence-led sections
01 / Concept clarification

Don't Rush to Count Companies: Neo Lab Is First of All a Floating Label

01

Before discussing "China's Neo Labs," you must separate the category, the companies, and the identically named project.

"Neolab" first became popular in the Silicon Valley investment context, used to observe a group of AI startups that had not yet formed significant revenue but earned high valuations because of researcher résumés, technical hypotheses, and talent density. Its list and definition expanded several times in a short period, showing that it is closer to an observation framework built by investors than a category established by academia, industry associations, or standards bodies. English-language media later interpreted it broadly as "startups engaged in foundational research," further weakening hard boundaries such as valuation, revenue, and development stage.

After entering the Chinese-language context, the term underwent two further expansions: financing coverage used "NEO Lab" to describe new companies led by researchers from universities or top labs and betting on undecided technical paradigms; the edited notes from Lu Qi's course theorized it into an organizational paradigm that places research, engineering, capital, product, and market inside the same organization to complete a "-1 to 1" loop. The latter definition can even extend to AI infrastructure, chips, robotics, and aerospace, and is already clearly broader than Silicon Valley's original "low-revenue, high-valuation, pre-product" framing.

One must also specifically distinguish the project literally named "Neo Lab" in a roadshow in Shanghai's Putuo District. Public materials show only that this project focuses on a "perception–modeling–decision" loop and plans to build an embodied-intelligence data collection center; they disclose no legal entity, founding team, or affiliation with other companies. For now it can only be regarded as an identically named, standalone project, and cannot be used to prove that a unified Neo Lab industry has emerged in China.

  • "Neo" has not been confirmed to be an acronym composed of three English words.
  • Neo Lab describes an organizational form, while Researcher Founder describes a founder's capability mix; the two are not equivalent.
  • Broadly defined "research-oriented AI companies" have long existed; what the new label truly highlights is capital taking on scientific risk in advance, when product and revenue evidence are still insufficient.

02 / Sample boundary

Under a Strict Definition There Is No Cohort Yet; Under a Broad Definition There Is Already a Batch of Research-Oriented Companies

02

Whether "China has already produced a large number of Neo Labs" holds true depends entirely on which yardstick you use.

If you follow the narrow definition, a company must simultaneously be researcher-led, have an original frontier model as its core asset, have not yet achieved PMF, have low annual revenue, reach unicorn valuation, and have a clear long-term technical goal. Based on the candidate pool built from the supplied materials, as of July 2026 there is not a single company in mainland China that can, on public information, simultaneously prove all of these conditions. This does not mean the actual number is necessarily zero, but rather that valuation, revenue, and PMF status are often undisclosed, and unknown fields should not be defaulted to "passing."

If you adopt a broader organizational definition—requiring only that research leadership hold real power, that original models constitute the core asset, and that a research–engineering–product feedback loop has formed—then roughly eight mainland organizations such as DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, MiniMax, Zhipu, Baichuan, StepFun, Shengshu Technology, and Galbot can enter the field of observation, with Inverse Matrix Technology (Nimble) tentatively listed as a candidate. But their development stages, capital structures, and technical targets differ greatly: some already have massive user bases, some serve large numbers of institutions, and some have already gone public or entered large-scale deployment. Calling all of them narrowly defined Neo Labs would erase the concept's most distinctive features of "pre-product, high scientific risk, capital-first."

Inverse Matrix Technology is currently the case closest to a Chinese narrow-definition sample: public reports show that it is composed of academic talent from Peking University and engineering talent from big tech companies, bets on world foundation models and reinforcement learning, and has secured a large early-stage financing round; its founder has also accepted the Neo Lab label bestowed by outsiders. But its valuation, revenue, customers, full technical reports, and independent reproduction materials remain insufficient, so a more accurate identity is "candidate Neo Lab" rather than an already-verified successful sample.

Overseas-linked samples should also be counted separately. Axiom Math, World Labs, and AMI Labs are connected to founders who grew up in mainland China or received higher education and research training there, but they are American or French organizations and cannot be written up as "Chinese companies." Among them, AMI Labs is closest to the strict definition; this difference also reminds us that when discussing "China's Neo Labs," one must distinguish a company's place of registration and operation, its founders' upbringing, and their ethnic identity.

  • The candidate pool is not an exhaustive census of Chinese AI companies.
  • Low revenue does not equal a lack of PMF, and high user volume does not equal PMF already achieved.
  • Mature research-oriented companies can serve as a control group for what a Neo Lab looks like after commercialization, but they should not be reverse-engineered into early-stage Neo Labs.

03 / Technical review

What Truly Opens Up the Gap Is Not the Lab's Name but an Attributable Technical Chain

03

Financing and PhD density can only show that a team has obtained resources; they cannot prove it has already created new technology.

On public technical evidence, China's samples already show clear stratification. DeepSeek and Moonshot AI best support the judgment of "research lab that is also a startup." DeepSeek pursues algorithm–system co-design around MLA, MoE routing, training precision, communication, and inference, and publicly releases weights, inference code, and technical reports; Moonshot AI's Mooncake, by contrast, comes from Kimi's real production constraints and has produced peer-reviewed serving-system research. Together the two show that Chinese research-oriented companies do not merely hand papers to product departments, but let production problems directly generate research topics.

Zhipu's early GLM pre-training objective has a clear, original academic contribution; MiniMax embodies notable scaling innovation in combining long context, linear attention, and large-scale MoE; and Baichuan has built differentiation in medical models and vertical validation. Their value should not be simply dismissed as "engineering assembly," because training stability, data loops, distributed systems, and reliable deployment are themselves technical barriers; but evaluation should still distinguish foundational-method originality, scaling innovation, and vertical integration, and should not let all subsequent models under the same brand automatically inherit the halo of early originality.

Open weights also do not equal full reproduction. Most mature samples have already reached "runnable," some projects can be "re-reviewed" using public scripts, and some methods can be redone on small-scale models; but training data, cleaning pipelines, the full training stack, reinforcement-learning environments, cluster fault handling, and comprehensive ablations usually remain invisible, and almost no company reaches full retrainability. The claim that "research results have already been validated by open source" must therefore specify exactly which layer they have been opened up to.

By comparison, the main evidence for Inverse Matrix and the identically named Shanghai project at present is still financing, roadmaps, demos, or construction plans. Lacking papers, code, model weights, standard benchmarks, and independent re-reviews clearly attributable to the new organization, they can only be regarded as research plans awaiting verification. Valuation, data-center planning, and the papers team members published in the past at universities or former employers cannot substitute for the new organization's own technical delivery.

  • Prioritize observing continuous, attributable methods and third-party re-reviews, rather than self-reported conclusions such as "globally leading."
  • The public release time from research to product can be near zero days, but the actual R&D cycle may still last months or years.
  • Product first spawning papers, with research and deployment happening in parallel, may describe this kind of organization better than the traditional "translate after the paper" model.

04 / Organizational capability

What Is Most Worth Studying About a Neo Lab Is Who Can Reallocate Research, Compute, and Product at the Same Table

04

Putting scientists, engineers, and product managers into one company is nothing new; the real proposition is decision rights and feedback speed.

Where a Neo Lab may differ from a university lab, a corporate research institute, and an ordinary AI startup is that research judgment, compute allocation, financing, and product cadence can be coordinated by the same founding layer. University labs can sustain long-cycle research and rely on peer review, but usually lack standing product, growth, and customer-success functions; ordinary startups prioritize eliminating demand risk and allocate resources around the MVP and PMF; corporate research institutes possess data, users, and engineering platforms, but research and product may belong to different budget centers. A Neo Lab attempts to place these advantages inside one organization while taking on all three risks of science, engineering, and market.

The team of twenty or thirty people that Inverse Matrix publicly describes presents a relatively pure early prototype: research and engineering staff are roughly evenly split, there are no formal departments or KPIs, small groups first make core breakthroughs and then conduct scale validation, and financing mainly serves compute and talent expansion. But public materials do not yet show stable user feedback, a product owner, or a revenue mechanism. Therefore, what it currently proves is more of a "research–engineering loop" than a complete "research–engineering–product loop."

Scaled-up companies provide another type of evidence. MiniMax's heads of research, vision models, product, and commercialization sit in the top governance layer; DeepSeek can simultaneously release technical reports, weights, an API, and a chat product; Zhipu demonstrates vertical integration from university research, a pre-training framework, and hardware adaptation to platforms and end products. But these facts can only prove the components or outcomes of the loop; they cannot fully reveal topic-selection rights, budget allocation, elimination of failed routes, and incentive mechanisms.

Therefore, whether a Neo Lab constitutes an independent organizational paradigm cannot be judged by a flat culture or a founder's halo. More explanatory indicators include: the median cycle for research results to enter usable products, the proportion of user failure cases that enter the next round of training, the speed of adjusting compute between exploration and production, the retention and equity coverage of research and engineering backbones, and whether cross-functional collaboration can continue when the founder is not personally involved. Only when these capabilities are consistently superior to those of alternative organizations over the long term does a Neo Lab become more than just a nice-sounding name.

  • The key to the loop is shared metrics, flowing experimental data, and the right to reallocate resources—not the elimination of all division of labor.
  • Research without user feedback degenerates into a capitalized lab.
  • If short-term product metrics completely override technical judgment, it converges toward an ordinary AI company.

05 / The China variable

The Chinese Version Won't Replicate Silicon Valley: It Is More Compute-Constrained and Closer to the Industrial Front Line

05

The prospects of China's Neo Labs depend on how local talent, manufacturing scenarios, policy capital, and compute constraints jointly interact.

China possesses several basic conditions for forming research-oriented startup organizations: AI papers, citations, patents, and significant model output constitute a fairly thick research foundation; the system for commercializing university research continues to loosen; and robotics, automobiles, manufacturing, and urban governance provide rich physical scenarios for world models and embodied intelligence. Public research platforms such as BAAI also serve a bridging function connecting universities, open-source infrastructure, research teams, and startups. This makes it possible for China's samples to face real systems, costs, and scenario feedback earlier than pure digital-model companies.

But total supply cannot be equated with the resources actually available to a startup. China's compute scale continues to grow, yet frontier GPUs, HBM, and manufacturing equipment are affected by export controls; startup teams rely more on cloud rental, adaptation to domestic chips, local public compute, existing institutional clusters, or historical inventory. Compute vouchers and model vouchers can reduce some marginal costs, but they cannot substitute for the long-term capital that covers years of training and trial and error. Data presents a similar contradiction: the total volume is enormous, but compliant, usable, high-quality Chinese corpora, industry data, and embodied data remain scarce.

Capital structure will likewise shape technical routes. Private AI investment in the United States is significantly deeper and can support enormous financing before a product appears; China relies more on industrial capital, local funds, state-owned funds, cloud vendors, or special parent entities. These can extend a company's runway, but may also demand local landing, domestic adaptation, and industrial metrics, pushing companies into engineering delivery earlier. DeepSeek, supported by a quant business and an existing compute system, is a capital structure difficult for ordinary startups to replicate.

This environment is not necessarily only a disadvantage. Compute scarcity may force stronger efficiency innovation, manufacturing scenarios may bring shorter physical feedback chains, and open models may broaden technical spillover. But it means that the evaluation criteria for China's Neo Labs should not merely replicate the valuations and founder résumés of star U.S. companies, but should examine whether they can form an independent, sustainable technology–product cycle under constrained conditions.

  • Policy subsidies can improve costs, but cannot substitute for sustainable financing and revenue.
  • Regulation mainly increases the data- and content-governance costs of public-facing products; it does not amount to a ban on internal research.
  • Local investment-promotion events can provide resources and scenarios, but a single signing cannot prove technical capability or a nationwide trend.

06 / Capital judgment

Recognize Research-Organization Innovation, but Apply Milestone-Based Discounting to Commercial Value

06

A Neo Lab can lose money for a long time, but cannot be exempt from verification indefinitely.

Foundation models, world models, and embodied intelligence do require expensive compute, high-density talent, and years of R&D, and cannot mechanically apply the short-term profit standards of an ordinary SaaS company. But when capital bets ahead of time while revenue and product evidence are still insufficient, valuation itself is no longer an outcome, but merely an option on a future position of technical monopoly. Successful financing only proves that a company can sell equity or debt; it cannot prove that the technical route, customer demand, or unit economics have been established.

The mature control group already shows varying degrees of commercial validation. MiniMax's disclosed revenue structure, overseas revenue, and gross-margin improvement make its commercial evidence relatively solid; Zhipu has already formed enterprise deployment, API, and agent revenue, but still has a strong project-delivery character, and its R&D spending far exceeds its revenue; Moonshot AI has consumer products and an API, but lacks sufficient audited data to judge gross margin, customer acquisition cost, and cash burn. They show that research-oriented companies can generate revenue, yet have not generally proven that revenue is sufficient to cover sustained large-model R&D.

The risk for early-stage candidate companies is more direct. Inverse Matrix's public evidence mainly includes team, financing, roadmap, and an initial model demo, while customers, pricing, revenue, repeat purchase, and production-environment validation are still absent. One cannot on this basis assert that it has a valuation bubble, because its valuation is not fully disclosed; but "financing faster than technical and customer validation" is itself a risk signal that should be recorded.

In judging whether a financing round is reasonable, the key is not whether the company is losing money, but whether it has eliminated an auditable risk: whether the technical results can be re-reviewed by third parties, whether the unit cost per task is falling, whether unaffiliated paying customers and repeat purchases have appeared, whether the cash runway can cover the next technical milestone, and whether the financing terms create excessive repayment or control pressure. China's Neo Labs deserve support, but the way to support them should be to release trust in stages, rather than pricing "could become a platform" ahead of time as "has already become a platform."

  • For early-stage companies, do not reject them outright for having no revenue, nor raise the probability of technical success because the financing amount is huge.
  • For mature companies, look at standardized revenue, gross margin, R&D efficiency, and capital burn—not registered users and model leaderboards.
  • Pivoting the research route, going vertical, or having the team acquired is not necessarily failure, but it does refute the narrative that "a general research route necessarily grows linearly."

Editor’s note
Evidence limits

  1. Evidence gap. No industry association, academic paper, or standards body provides a unified definition of "Neo Lab," so the sample count depends heavily on the research definition used.
  2. Evidence gap. The "earliest source" can only be stated as a relatively early usage within the currently retrieved materials; LinkedIn, WeChat public accounts, and closed-door events may not be fully indexed.
  3. Evidence gap. Lu Qi's course relies mainly on public text edited and compiled by the media; no official permanent page or complete verbatim transcript of the same version was found.
  4. Evidence gap. The valuation, revenue, PMF, customer repeat purchase, financing terms, and cash burn of many private companies are not public, so "0 confirmable companies" under the strict definition represents insufficient evidence and does not mean the actual number is necessarily zero.
  5. Evidence gap. Inverse Matrix's financing and technical materials come mainly from the company, investment institutions, or deal-related coverage; independent technical re-review is still insufficient.
  6. Evidence gap. The Neo Lab in the Shanghai roadshow lacks a legal entity, team, financing, model, and follow-up landing materials, and cannot be linked to Neo Lab in the categorical sense.
  7. Evidence gap. Mature companies' technical reports, financial disclosures, and website materials can prove some facts, but company-selected benchmarks, user metrics, and internal ways of working may still involve selective disclosure.
  8. Evidence gap. The candidate pool is composed of companies that have received financing and media exposure, which creates survivorship bias and cannot be used to estimate the success rate of the entire cohort of Chinese research-oriented AI startups.
